      📐 Why tilted armor is stronger

      A shell flies in a straight line. If the plate is tilted, the shell has to travel through more metal to get out the other side.

      ↗ Ricochets

      If the plate is tilted far enough, the shell can skip off like a stone on water. Each shell type has its own limit:

        Overmatch: if a solid shell is more than 3 times wider than the plate is thick, it just bites in and can't bounce.

        🚀 Shell types

          🧱 Special armor

          • Composite: layers of ceramic and other materials. Much stronger than steel for its thickness, especially against shaped charges.
          • Spaced armor: a thin plate with an air gap behind it. It sets off HEAT and HESH early, so the jet weakens and the squash head is wasted.
          • Reactive tiles: boxes that explode outward when hit. They wreck shaped-charge jets and shove solid shells a little.
          • Aluminium: light and easy to move around, but you need about 3 times more of it to stop the same shell.

          📏 Distance

          Solid shells (AP, APCR, APDS and darts) slow down in the air, so they punch less from far away. Light shells like APCR slow down the fastest.

          HEAT, HESH and HE do their work with an explosion when they hit, so distance doesn't change how much they can get through.

          🎯 Target range tips

          • Turn on Show weak spots to see where your shell can get through.
          • Turning a tank makes its front armor tilted, so it's harder to get through, but it shows more of its side.
          • Lower front plates, gun mantlets and roofs are often weaker.
          • Hitting the tracks stops the target from moving. Hitting the barrel damages its gun.

          🧪 How the Sandbox works

          Everything in the Sandbox is made of thousands of particles. Each one has a real material's weight and stiffness and is joined to its neighbors by tiny springs called bonds.

          • Bend: push a bond past the metal's strength and it bends for good (that's a dent).
          • Snap: stretch it too far and it breaks (that's a crack or a hole).
          • Flow: soft metals re-join as they squeeze past each other, so they flow like hot clay. Brittle things like ceramic and glass just crack.
          • Heat: bending and rubbing turn into heat, so the crater glows. Hot metal gets weaker.

          Nothing is animated by hand: holes, spall, ricochets and the shaped-charge jet all come from the physics, a few million times a second of simulated time.

          📐 Why "2D slice"?

          The Sandbox simulates a thin slice through the middle of the shell and the armor. Real armor is 3D, and the metal all around the hole holds it in place, so the Sandbox adds a 3D grip to make up for the missing direction.

          Its strength was tuned so solid shells land close to the classic De Marre formula (for example, a 75 mm shell at 620 m/s stops at about 100 mm of steel), and steep plates were tuned to match WWII slope tests: an 88 mm shell that beats 154 mm of flat steel only beats about 72 mm sloped at 55°. Full-size shells and shaped charges land within about 10% of the classic numbers; thin sub-calibre cores (APCR, APDS) come out lower. It's a physics toy, not real data.

          🚜 Vehicles and the builder

          Pick Vehicles in the Sandbox to shoot at side cut-aways of 26 real vehicles (tanks, tank hunters, an infantry carrier and even a tractor with a steel box bolted on, from 1941 to today) and 2 made-up ones. The whole vehicle is made of particles, tens of thousands of them. Most of them "sleep" until something hits them, which keeps it fast. After the hit, the shell and every bit of armor it knocked loose fly on through the inside:

          • 🧑 Crew get hurt or knocked out. If more than half are out, the vehicle is knocked out.
          • 💣 Ammo can explode: the blast throws the turret off and tears armor loose, and the pieces fly and land with gravity. Blow-out panels send the blast outside instead.
          • ⛽ Fuel can catch fire, and a wrecked ⚙️ engine or gearbox stops it moving.
          • APHE shells carry a little explosive that goes off just after they get inside.
          • Where things sit matters. The Merkava has its engine in the front, so a shot has to get through it to reach the crew. The T-14's crew sit in an armored capsule away from the ammo, so they can live through it blowing up.

          Damage stays, so you can keep shooting the same vehicle: a hole lets the next shell straight through. 🔧 Repair makes it good as new.

          To keep it quick, full physics runs for the first 1.2 milliseconds after the hit. After that, anything still flying carries on in a straight line as one solid lump, and flying wreckage is slowed down so you can watch it. The real tanks follow their real outlines, gun lengths, wheels and cupolas, with rough armor numbers from public sources; their insides are simplified. With Build a new one you can draw your own vehicle, save it, and send it to a friend with a link.

          🚀 Rockets, uranium and sandbags

          • Rockets and missiles (bazooka, Panzerfaust, RPG-7, wire-guided missile) carry shaped charges, so their speed doesn't matter. The tandem missile fires a small charge first to set off reactive armor, then the main jet goes through.
          • The top-attack slug is a lump of copper shaped by an explosion, flying at 2 km/s. Try it on a tank from above, where the armor is thin.
          • Uranium darts are heavier than tungsten and shed metal instead of flattening, so the same dart goes a bit deeper.
          • Sand, water and brick are weak but thick. A shaped-charge jet goes about three times deeper in water than in steel, and sandbags stop bullets.
          • Everyday things: try a pistol bullet on a car door or a police shield, or see whether an engine block stops rifle fire.

          ⚡ Two ways to solve a shot

          The Sandbox can work a shot out two ways. Pick one under Solver.

          • 🧪 Particles is the full physics: every bit of shell and armor moves on its own. It's slower, but you see it happen.
          • ⚡ Formulas gives the answer instantly with the equations real terminal-ballistics tools use, plate by plate. Darts follow the Alekseevskii–Tate model: the rod's nose wears away as it pushes the armor aside. Shells are solid bodies slowed by the armor's resistance, and thin plates get a plug punched out. Rosenberg's rule says when a dart bounces off, Recht–Ipson gives the exit speed from the speed needed to get through, and Grady's formula estimates the fragments behind the armor.

          For each plate the formulas table shows how much of the shell wore away, the slowest speed that would still get through (the "ballistic limit"), and the speed coming out. After a particle shot, open What the formulas say to compare the two.

          ☢️ Strange darts and old-time guns

          The strange darts are the 550 mm tungsten dart made of other things. Plutonium, gold and osmium all go about as deep as tungsten (roughly 500 mm of steel). At 1.6 km/s a dart flows almost like a liquid, so what counts most is how dense it is, and these are all about as dense. Tungsten is a little better because it's also strong and hard to melt.

          • Diamond is the hardest thing there is, but it's light and brittle, so it shatters against 100 mm of steel.
          • No, the plutonium dart doesn't go nuclear. That takes plutonium squeezed together in a very precise way, and a dart just smashes apart. It would leave hot, poisonous, radioactive bits that catch fire in air, which is why nobody would ever make one.
          • Old-time guns: a musket ball goes straight through a knight's breastplate. A 32-pounder cannonball goes through a warship's oak side, and a 12-pounder sends splinters flying off the inside.
          • Pykrete is ice mixed with sawdust. In WWII it was tested for a giant ship made of ice, and it stops a rifle bullet that goes straight through plain ice.

          🎯 Several shots at once

          Under Shots at once you can fire 2, 3, 5 or 8 at the same time. Side by side sends them in together, each on its own line, so they hit a row of spots at once. One after another lines them up nose to tail, like a burst from a machine gun, so each one hits the hole the last one made.

          Each shot plays out on its own, and the result counts them up ("3 of 5 got through"). In a row, the next shot comes in as soon as the last one has finished, so you don't sit through the empty flight in between. Lots of tiny bullets take the longest, since they need the smallest particles.

          🧮 How the Calculator works

          This is a simplified, game-style model, like the ones tank games use. Full-size solid shells use the De Marre formula from 1886. Long darts use a simple "wears away as it goes" estimate. Shaped charges get a set amount of penetration for their size.

          Real shells and armor are much more complicated, so the numbers here are for fun and learning, not real data. The vehicles on the target range are made up.
